# OEM Identification

## When to Use

The buyer has a product sold under a brand, distributor, or importer name and wants to know who makes it, or wants to confirm a vendor is the factory and not a reseller.

## Procedure

1. Collect what the buyer has: product photos (all markings, labels, packaging, date and lot codes), model number, catalog page, SDS, certificates, and invoices. Use only the company's own copies; do not upload customer drawings.
2. Work the evidence trail; record each lead with its source and strength (strong: regulator or registry record; medium: import records, patents; weak: lookalike listings):
   - Safety listings name the manufacturer and often the factory: UL Product iQ (file number on the label), Intertek ETL Directory, CSA, TÜV certificates, NSF listings.
   - Radio and electrical products: FCC ID grantee code (FCC equipment authorization database) names the applicant; test reports often name the factory.
   - Fasteners: head markings registered under the Fastener Quality Act insignia records identify the manufacturer or importer.
   - Chemicals, adhesives, lubricants, rubber compounds: SDS section 1 names the supplier; compare to the original formulator's SDS.
   - US import records: the brand appears as consignee; the shipper is often the factory or its trading arm. Match product description, HS code, and timing.
   - Patents and trademarks: Google Patents, USPTO, CNIPA, JPO assignees; trademark owner vs licensee.
   - Regulatory filings: DOT plant codes (tires), FDA establishment registration where relevant, EPA, CPSC recalls naming the manufacturer.
   - Lookalike listings: the same model number, mold marks, or packaging on Alibaba, Made-in-China, or factory websites. Weak alone; useful to confirm.
3. Triangulate: name an OEM only when two independent medium-or-strong sources agree. Otherwise report ranked candidates.
4. Run vendor-due-diligence and trade-compliance-screen on the OEM before any approach.
5. Note commercial implications: whether the OEM sells direct to distributors, MOQ, whether the brand owner holds exclusivity, tooling, or IP that prevents direct buying.

## Pitfalls

- Buying the same part direct can still infringe the brand owner's trademark, design patent, or tooling ownership. Flag for review before switching.
- A shipper on a bill of lading may be a forwarder or consolidator, not the maker.
- Sourcing the OEM of a product a customer buys from the company can trigger contractual restrictions; check the customer agreement.

## Verification

Report lists the OEM (or ranked candidates), each with independent sources, strength, and the IP or contract issues to clear.
